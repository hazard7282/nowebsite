Why Spotify Playlists Are the Fastest Way to Grow Your Fanbase (And How to Actually Get On Them)

By Adam Bogdan  ·  Rapture's Deli  ·  2026

If you're an independent artist trying to grow your streams, you've probably heard it a hundred times: get on playlists. It's repeated so often it's almost become a cliché — but the reason it keeps coming up is simple. Landing on the right Spotify playlists, whether editorial or independently curated, is still one of the most reliable ways to reach new listeners who have never heard of you.

This article breaks down how playlist placement works, why editorial playlists are so hard to crack, and how curator pitching services like Submithub, Playlist Push, and Groover can be used the right way to improve your odds.

Why Playlists Move the Needle

A single placement on a mid-sized playlist can expose your track to thousands of listeners who follow that playlist specifically because they trust its curation. Unlike a random social post, playlist listeners are already primed to discover new music in that genre — which tends to make playlist traffic convert into real fans rather than passive scrollers.

This matters for music marketing because streaming isn't just about one big spike. It's about building momentum: more streams lead to more saves, more saves lead to more algorithmic recommendations, and the cycle compounds. That's what most artists really mean when they say they want to "grow their fanbase" — not one viral moment, but a steady climb in listeners and streams over time.

Editorial Playlists: Powerful, But Extremely Competitive

Spotify's own editorial playlists — the ones curated by Spotify's in-house team, like genre-specific flagship playlists — carry enormous weight. Getting featured on one can meaningfully move your popularity index and unlock further algorithmic visibility. The catch is that acceptance rates are, by most independent artist accounts, very low. You submit through Spotify for Artists at least a week before release, and the reality is that the overwhelming majority of pitches are never accepted.

I can't point you to an official, published acceptance-rate statistic — Spotify hasn't released exact numbers publicly, as far as I'm aware — but anecdotal reporting from artists and industry blogs consistently describes it as a long shot. Treat any specific percentage you see quoted online with some skepticism unless it links back to Spotify directly.

Apple Music's editorial team works similarly: human curators, high standards, and no guarantees.

Using Curator Services the Right Way

This is where third-party playlist pitching services come in. Submithub, Playlist Push, and Groover connect artists with independent curators who run playlists across Spotify, YouTube, and other platforms. Used carelessly, these platforms can feel like a lottery. Used strategically, they can be genuinely effective.

The difference comes down to research. Before submitting:

  • Study the curator's existing playlists. Listen through them. Does your track's tempo, energy, and production style actually fit?
  • Match genre and subgenre precisely. A "deep house" curator and a "tech house" curator may look similar on paper but curate very differently.
  • Read curator feedback history, when available on these platforms, to see how they've responded to similar tracks in the past.
  • Avoid mass-submitting to every curator on the platform. A handful of well-matched pitches tends to outperform dozens of mismatched ones.

The underlying logic is straightforward: when your music consistently lands on playlists that fit its sound, it generates stronger engagement signals — saves, completed listens, follows. Spotify has never officially published the full mechanics of how these signals feed into its own algorithmic playlists like Discover Weekly and Release Radar. But it's a pattern that a lot of independent artists have observed firsthand: releases that pick up real engagement on independent or curator-run playlists tend to see a corresponding lift in algorithmic playlist placements shortly after.

That's not a guarantee, and results vary by genre and release, but it's a consistent enough pattern across enough artists' experiences — including my own — that it's worth planning around, even without an official confirmation from Spotify.

Putting It Together

Playlist growth isn't a single tactic — it's a layered strategy:

  1. Pitch directly to Spotify and Apple editorial teams for every release.
  2. Use curator services like Submithub, Playlist Push, or Groover to reach independent playlists that fit your sound.
  3. Research curators before pitching instead of submitting blindly.
  4. Let strong engagement on independent playlists build the signals that may help you reach Spotify's own algorithmic playlists over time.

None of this guarantees results, and any claims of "guaranteed" placement should be treated as a red flag. But approached with patience and genre-accurate targeting, playlist pitching remains one of the most effective ways an independent artist can grow real, lasting streams — not just a temporary bump, but a foundation for long-term growth.
